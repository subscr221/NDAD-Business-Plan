# SCALE UAS: An Investor Business Case

**Class:** Specialised C5ISR Autonomous Long Endurance (SCALE) unmanned aircraft systems
**Anchor market:** India, with an export pathway after a domestic reference
**Audience:** A defense-literate lead investor and generalist co-investors
**Nature of the numbers:** Market figures are sourced ranges. The entrant scenario in §7 is one internally consistent illustration, built bottom-up from a transaction-validated price band. It is not a forecast, and no figure in this document comes from any single company's model.

> **Reading the acronyms.** C5ISR stands for Command, Control, Computers, Communications, Cyber, Intelligence, Surveillance and Reconnaissance. In plain terms: the network of sensors, radios, computers and decision aids through which a military force sees the battlefield and directs its assets. A SCALE UAS is a large unmanned aircraft, in the one-to-five-tonne bracket, that stays aloft for 24 to 40 hours and does C5ISR work: it carries the radar, collects the signals, relays the communications, runs the mission computers, and increasingly directs smaller drones. Think of it less as an aircraft with sensors bolted on and more as a data centre and radio mast that happens to fly.

---

## Prologue: The Signature That Started the Clock

Begin where the customer begins, on the water. India looks out on 7,500 kilometres of coastline and an exclusive economic zone of 2.4 million square kilometres, an expanse through which Chinese survey ships, submarines and task groups now move as a matter of routine. An ocean that size cannot be watched from a pier. It must be watched from altitude, for days at a time, by something that carries a radar worth the name.

In 2024 the Government of India signed for 31 MQ-9B aircraft at roughly USD 4 billion, and the Indian Navy, the service with the sharpest picture of its own hunger, took the largest share of the order. That signature is the inciting incident of this entire case. It settled, at a government-published price, three arguments that pitches usually ask you to take on faith: that the demand is real, that the mission is maritime, and that the customer will pay ₹150 to 210 Cr per aircraft system for the privilege of seeing its ocean. India's stated requirement for the decade runs to 70 to 100 aircraft in this class. The clock on that requirement is now running, and this document exists to help you judge every company that comes asking to fill it.

## 1. Two Businesses Wearing One Name

Ten years ago an investor reading a pitch for a large military drone would have filed it under one label: MALE, medium-altitude long-endurance, the class the MQ-9 Reaper made famous. That label is now doing too much work. It bundles together two businesses with opposite fates, and the first task of diligence in this sector is telling them apart.

The first business is the armed drone loitering over a contested battlefield, and Ukraine wrote its ending. The Bayraktar TB2 went from cover-story icon to routine air-defense kill inside eighteen months. Houthi missiles costing a fraction of an MQ-9 have brought down more than a dozen of them over the Red Sea since 2023. A slow aircraft the size of a small airliner does not survive over a defended battlefield, and no amount of engineering spend changes that arithmetic. Pitches still circulate for penetrating strike drones in this class. Decline them.

The second business is the one this document makes the case for, and it thrives on the very qualities that doomed the first. Size, endurance, power generation and payload volume make the class hopeless over a defended battlefield and irreplaceable in a different role: the persistent, high-flying node that watches an ocean, drinks in signals from hundreds of kilometres away, relays communications for forces beyond the horizon, and manages flocks of cheaper expendable drones. Militaries have started to procure for this role explicitly. The aircraft is bought for its aperture and its endurance, and it earns its keep at standoff range, where the missile threat thins out.

SCALE, Specialised C5ISR Autonomous Long Endurance, names that second business so an investor can evaluate it on its own terms. Each of the four words carries weight.

**Specialised.** The aircraft is designed around specific C5ISR missions, maritime surveillance, signals intelligence, communications relay, battle management, rather than as a general-purpose bomb truck with a camera. Specialisation shows up in the engineering: power budgets sized for radar, antenna apertures designed in from the start, open payload bays, satellite datalinks as a core requirement rather than an option.

**C5ISR.** The revenue-bearing content of the aircraft sits in its mission systems, the radar, the signals payloads, the mission computers, the datalinks and the software that fuses what they collect. The airframe carries this content the way a rack carries servers. Whoever owns the mission stack owns the margin, a point this document returns to at length in §6.

**Autonomous.** The aircraft flies itself for most of the sortie, manages its own sensors against a tasking order, and needs a small crew supervising several aircraft rather than a large crew flying one. Autonomy is what separates this class economically from the crewed maritime patrol aircraft it partially replaces: a P-8I costs several times as much per flying hour, and its crew of nine can stay airborne for a third as long. Autonomy also underwrites the emerging mothership role, in which the SCALE aircraft launches and directs expendable drones. A human stays on the loop for any use of force; the point of autonomy here is persistence and crew economics, never lethal independence.

**Long Endurance.** Sorties of 24 to 40 hours. Endurance is what turns three or four aircraft into a continuous orbit a thousand kilometres from the coast, and it rests on fuel fraction and wing design, qualities that do not miniaturise. This is the physical moat around the class, and §4 walks through why cheap small drones cannot cross it.

## 2. The Governing Thesis

**Certification capacity, not demand, is the scarce asset in the SCALE class. The entrant that converts sovereign demand into certified backlog earliest captures a decade of high-price, low-volume aircraft revenue plus a 25-year annuity in mission systems, software and sustainment. The airframe is the entry ticket. The C5ISR stack is the business.**

Three pillars carry this claim, and the rest of the document evidences each in turn:

1. **Demand is real, sovereign-backed and mission-validated.** India signed for 31 MQ-9B aircraft in 2024 at roughly USD 4 billion, and the Indian Navy, the customer with the sharpest picture of its own needs, took the largest share. India's stated requirement for the decade runs to 70 to 100 aircraft in this class. The 2024 transaction settles the demand argument with a government-published price, and the naval weighting of the order tells you which missions the customer values. India bought this class for the ocean.
2. **Certification filters the field before the market does.** Military airworthiness certification for an aircraft this size takes five to seven years and consumes capital before meaningful product revenue exists. Few entrants can finance the wait. The toll gate turns a crowded field of aspirants into an oligopoly of two or three winners per sovereign market, and for the investor who backs a crossing entrant, the toll gate is the moat.
3. **The economics reward the platform-owner.** A SCALE aircraft in service generates sustainment, software and upgrade revenue of roughly 8 to 12 percent of fleet value per year across a service life of 25 years. The entrant that owns the mission computer, the autonomy stack and the payload interfaces collects that annuity. The entrant that assembles an airframe around licensed foreign sensors pays rent on it instead.

This is a sector case. It names no company, and every section closes with a short list of things to verify in any data room, consolidated into a scorecard in §10. Its job is to equip you to judge every SCALE pitch that crosses your desk, including the ones that call themselves MALE.

> **How defense procurement differs from what you already know.** One customer per country, the ministry of defense. Sales cycles of three to seven years. Prices are public more often than in commercial markets, because parliaments ask.

**What to verify:** market-share assumptions against the 70–100 aircraft national requirement; pricing inside the transaction-validated band; whether the pitch distinguishes the durable C5ISR missions from the disrupted strike mission, or leans on sizzle reels of weapons releases.

## 4. The Moat Is Physics: A Siege of Weight

The objection you will hear in every partner meeting runs like this: Ukraine proved that small, cheap, attritable drones do the work, so why pay ₹180 Cr for an aircraft when ₹18 lakh buys a swarm? The objection deserves a full answer, because the answer is where the thesis earns its conviction.

Return to the stage. India's defining surveillance problem is an ocean of 2.4 million square kilometres beyond 7,500 kilometres of coast. Now ask what a quadcopter does 1,200 kilometres from Kochi. It cannot get there. If it could get there, it could not carry a radar worth the name. If it could carry the radar, it could not power it. And if it could power it, it could not stay on station long enough to matter, because a small airframe spends most of its fuel getting to and from the patrol area. Four failures, each fatal on its own, stacked in a row.

Each of those failures traces to a physical quantity that refuses to shrink. A sea-search radar needs antenna area and kilowatts, which set a floor on airframe size and generator capacity. A thirty-hour orbit needs fuel fraction, which is a function of wing design and gross weight. A beyond-horizon track needs a satellite dish with real diameter. Sensor electronics ride Moore's law; apertures, fuel and antennas do not. This is a siege measured in weight rather than in numbers, and it is why the endurance moat has held through two decades of small-drone progress. Crossing it requires a small drone to stop being small.

Run the arithmetic on a single standing patrol. One continuous orbit 1,000 kilometres offshore takes three to four aircraft of 30-plus-hour endurance rotating on station, a slow ballet of relief and return. Covering the same water with five-hour small drones is never a matter of buying ten times as many; the transit time consumes their endurance before they reach station, so the answer diverges toward an unworkable logistics chain delivering no radar coverage at all. On cost per surveilled square kilometre per hour over open water, the large aircraft wins by orders of magnitude, and the Indian Navy's weighting of the MQ-9B order shows the customer has done this arithmetic too.

Now place the class inside the attritable-drone era rather than against it. In the kill chains taking shape, cheap expendable drones are the fists. Something has to be the eyes and the nervous system: the platform that finds and tracks at standoff range, relays communications to assets beyond the horizon, and carries the computing to fuse it all. Those functions concentrate in exactly the SCALE class. Ukraine's celebrated naval-drone strikes, the favourite exhibit of the swarm argument, depended on persistent standoff surveillance to find and fix their targets. The attritable revolution does not delete the large aircraft. It promotes it, from hunter to hunt-master, and hunt-master is the better business: higher software content, deeper integration into the customer's command system, stickier revenue.

The honest converse deserves its sentence: the attritables reshape the product. The SCALE aircraft of 2030 launches expendable effects from its own wings, directs them mid-mission, and serves as the communications bridge between a swarm and a command post. General Atomics is already integrating air-launched effects on the MQ-9B, and Baykar markets the Akıncı as a controller. An entrant designing a closed-architecture aircraft today, sensors welded to airframe, software sealed by the vendor, is building a museum piece. Open payload bays and software-defined mission systems are a design requirement of the class, and any illustrative economics that assume otherwise are fiction.

**What to verify:** mission set weighted toward maritime surveillance, standoff signals collection, relay and drone-controller roles; open-architecture payload and autonomy roadmap with named interface standards; a management team that can articulate where the swarm argument wins and what they concede to it.

## 5. The Dragon at the Gate: Certification

> **Military airworthiness certification in plain terms.** Before an armed force accepts a large unmanned aircraft over its territory and its ships, a national airworthiness authority must certify the design, the software and the production process: design reviews, ground rigs, and hundreds to thousands of flight-test hours. In India the design authority is CEMILAC and the quality authority is DGAQA. Export customers impose their own equivalents, so certification is a gate an entrant passes more than once.

Every treasure worth having sits behind a guardian, and the guardian of this sector is certification. It organises everything, and an investor should hold three facts about it at once.

First, the dragon demands tribute in years and capital. Certification is a five-to-seven-year toll gate, and capital pays the toll before product revenue exists. Design, prototype, flight test, qualification: the sequence cannot be compressed by enthusiasm, and the regional precedent is sobering. India's indigenous Tapas program has run more than a decade without reaching series production. The business you are evaluating is, before anything else, a business of financing a wait, and the winner is the entrant whose balance sheet and institutional patience outlast the queue.

Second, the dragon is the reason the treasure exists at all. Because few entrants can finance the wait, each sovereign market ends up with two or three certified suppliers, and the certificate itself becomes the barrier that keeps late arrivals out. The gate is the filter, and the filter is the moat. An investor who backs a crossing entrant is buying a position on the far side, which is why every serious diligence question in this sector reduces to some version of: where is this company on the certification path, and how do you know?

Third, schedule slip on this path is the dominant value destroyer, ahead of any cost overrun. At catalogue prices of ₹150–210 Cr per aircraft, a twelve-month slip defers roughly ₹180 to 540 Cr of annual deliveries at the ramp rates in §7, and slips of that size are common in first-of-class programs. Price the slip when you price the round.

For the SCALE class specifically, the dragon has grown a second head: the autonomy and mission software must be certified alongside the airframe, under standards such as DO-178C for airborne software. This raises the toll, and it also deepens the moat for the entrant that builds a certification-grade software organisation early, because that organisation is as hard to replicate as the flight-test data itself.

**What to verify:** certification milestones achieved versus claimed, with dates; flight-test hours logged on representative prototypes rather than subscale demonstrators; a named, documented engagement with CEMILAC and DGAQA or their export equivalents, because a schedule without a named authority relationship is fiction; the software certification bench, in-house or through named partners.

## 6. Hero and Shadow: The Platform-Owner and the Shell-Maker

Two kinds of company will show up in your deal flow wearing the same class label. They look alike in a pitch deck. They photograph identically beside their prototypes. One is the hero of this story and the other is its shadow, and the distinction between them decides who collects the annuity.

The **platform-owner** holds the airframe IP, the flight-control software, the mission computer, the payload interfaces and the autonomy stack. When the customer wants a new radar mode, the platform-owner writes it and bills for it. When the fleet reaches mid-life, the platform-owner sells the upgrade program into its own installed base. Every sensor added to the aircraft deepens the customer's dependence on integration work only the platform-owner can do, which is the only durable answer to the extreme buyer power of a one-customer market.

The **shell-maker** builds or assembles the airframe and integrates licensed foreign sensors around a mission system it does not own. Assembly margin is real money, and shell-makers win contracts, sometimes splendidly. Then the sensor OEM sells the upgrades, the software house bills the change requests, and the shell-maker watches the annuity flow past its door like a river it can see and cannot drink from. Worse, the shell-maker is displaceable: any competitor with the same licences can offer the same aircraft, so each recompete is fought on price.

For the SCALE class the distinction sharpens into a test, because the C5ISR content is most of the value. An aircraft whose purpose is computing, communications and sensing, built by a company that owns none of the computing, communications or sensing, is a contradiction an investor should not fund.

The annuity deserves numbers, because it is the quiet fortune in this tale. Across defense aerospace, sustainment, spares, training, software support and periodic upgrades run at roughly 8 to 12 percent of installed fleet value per year, checkable against published sustainment contracts, and service lives in this class run 25 years. On the illustrative fleet in §7, fourteen aircraft at ₹180 Cr, the annuity reaches ₹200 to 300 Cr of recurring annual revenue by Year 10, before any mid-life upgrade program, and it compounds with every aircraft delivered. A pitch that leads with fourteen units sounds like a hobby. A pitch that leads with a 25-year mission-systems annuity per aircraft, secured by switching costs the customer cannot cheaply escape, is a business. The difference between the two pitches is which archetype is talking.

**What to verify:** IP ownership in substance, never in marketing language, across airframe, flight controls, mission computer and autonomy; the import share of the bill of materials, which measures both FX exposure and how much of the stack is rented; software headcount as a fraction of engineering, because a platform-owner in this class looks half like a software company on the org chart.

## 7. The First Decade, Told Honestly: One Internally Consistent Scenario

Everything in this section is an illustration, never a forecast. Its purpose is to show the shape of a credible SCALE entrant's first decade so you can stress any real pitch against it. Three construction rules keep it honest, and the same rules make a useful audit of anyone else's model: no product revenue before the first delivery; every revenue figure derived from units times price rather than asserted; backlog derived from a stated order cadence rather than drawn as a curve.

Assumptions: midpoint price ₹180 Cr per aircraft system, the centre of the transaction-validated band; first delivery in Year 4, consistent with a Year 1 start and a credible certification path; sustainment, software support and spares at 10 percent of installed fleet value per year, beginning the year after each delivery; orders booked from Year 2 as the anchor customer converts trials into contracts.

| ₹ Cr | Y1 | Y2 | Y3 | Y4 | Y5 | Y6 | Y7 | Y8 | Y9 | Y10 |
| ---- | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| Units ordered | 0 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| Units delivered | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| Aircraft revenue | 0 | 0 | 0 | 180 | 180 | 360 | 360 | 360 | 540 | 540 |
| Sustainment & software | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 18 | 36 | 72 | 108 | 144 | 198 |
| **Total revenue** | **0** | **0** | **0** | **180** | **198** | **396** | **432** | **468** | **684** | **738** |
| Cumulative fleet | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 11 | 14 |
| Closing backlog, units | 0 | 3 | 6 | 8 | 11 | 13 | 15 | 17 | 18 | 19 |
| Closing backlog, ₹ Cr | 0 | 540 | 1,080 | 1,440 | 1,980 | 2,340 | 2,700 | 3,060 | 3,240 | 3,420 |

Read the shape before the totals, the way you would read weather before a voyage. Three years of zero product revenue while orders accumulate, the silence before the harvest. A first delivery in Year 4 that turns on the annuity as well as the hardware line. From Year 6, sustainment and software growing faster than aircraft revenue in percentage terms, on their way to a fleet-sized annuity that will outlast the delivery ramp by two decades. And a closing backlog near five years of forward revenue, which is what actually gets the next round priced. If a customer funds development milestones in Years 1 to 3, show that as its own labelled line; the moment a model books it as product revenue, you are looking at a red flag.

> **The pre-build test.** Book-to-bill is orders booked divided by revenue recognised in a period. A SCALE entrant needs an order book of roughly three times its first-year delivery capacity before the first aircraft ships; in this scenario, six units ordered by end of Year 3 against one delivered in Year 4. An entrant arriving at first delivery without that pre-built backlog is asking you to fund certification risk and market risk at the same time. Treat the pre-build as a requirement to check, and be suspicious of a model that presents it as an achievement already banked.

> **The BoM-margin trap.** Hardware pitches in this sector love to quote gross margin on bill of materials alone, parts cost against price, which produces figures near 70 percent. Between the parts bin and a delivered, accepted aircraft sit assembly labour, integration, flight-test acceptance, warranty reserves, program management and amortised development cost. A fully loaded gross margin of 35 to 50 percent at maturity is the credible band for this class. A pitch quoting above it owes you the bridge, line by line.

One sensitivity dominates every other, and it is the dragon's shadow falling across the spreadsheet: shift the certification date twelve months to the right and every column above moves with it, deferring ₹180 to 540 Cr of annual revenue at ramp. No plausible cost overrun destroys as much value. When you model the downside case, model the slip.

**What a credible capital plan looks like**, staged so each round buys a falsifiable milestone rather than runway in the abstract:

| Stage | Buys | The milestone that unlocks the next stage |
| ----- | ---- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Series A | Flying prototype and the core mission-systems and autonomy team | First flight of a representative prototype; certification authority engagement on the record |
| Series B | Qualification, the flight-test campaign, first deliveries | Certification milestones passed on schedule; the anchor order converted from intent to contract |
| Growth | Rate production and export campaigns | Domestic reference fleet in service; export letters of intent in hand |

A contingency of 25 to 30 percent on non-recurring engineering is the credible norm for a first-of-class aircraft with certified software; a plan without it is optimistic by construction, and for a SCALE entrant the software qualification line deserves its own contingency rather than a share of the airframe's.

**What to verify:** the pitch's model against the three construction rules above; order-cadence assumptions tied to named customer programs rather than a market-share percentage; margin definition, fully loaded versus BoM; NRE contingency present and at 25 percent or better.

## 8. The Road Has Been Walked: Go-to-Market and the Queue

The demonstrated route to venture-scale returns in this sector has one shape, and Turkey's Baykar walked every step of it in living memory. A domestic anchor order first, the TB2 into Turkish service in 2014. Then combat validation, in Syria, Libya, Nagorno-Karabakh and Ukraine. Then an export flywheel now spanning more than thirty customer countries, with exports the majority of revenue. The sequence matters more than the company. Domestic reference first, exports second, and the domestic reference is what converts a brochure into a product that other governments will buy. The road exists. It has been survived. That is the comfort.

Two implications follow for a SCALE entrant, and both cut.

The favourable one: India's indigenisation regime, positive-indigenisation lists and import-substitution mandates, keeps closing the home market to foreign airframes, which protects the window in which a domestic entrant can win the anchor order. The 2024 MQ-9B buy reads best as a stopgap purchased while the domestic pipeline matures, and the stated ambition on the record remains a sovereign platform.

The unfavourable one: the road is narrow and crowded, because every credible entrant in every producing country is standing in the same queue. The domestic reference is a winner-take-most tournament, two or three certified winners per sovereign market and a long tail of also-rans whose investors financed flight-test programs for nothing. This is why §5's certification questions and §3's named-customer questions are the heart of the diligence, and why traction claims should be discounted to the level of documentary evidence behind them.

For the SCALE class the export argument carries one extra edge worth naming in an IC memo: navies are the natural first export customers. Maritime surveillance is the mission where the class is unanswerable, coastal states from the Gulf to Southeast Asia share India's ocean-awareness problem at smaller scale, and a naval-reference fleet travels well diplomatically, since a maritime patrol aircraft raises fewer end-use objections than an armed strike platform.

**What to verify:** named anchor-customer engagement, letters of intent, trial participation or development contracts, never "pipeline"; the entrant's position in the domestic queue relative to named rivals; an export thesis that starts with navies and permissive-airspace customers rather than a coloured map.

## 9. The Watchfires: Risks, and What Would Kill the Thesis

Every long campaign posts sentries. Here is the register, and after it, the four fires to watch through the night.

| Risk | Weight | The mitigant to look for |
| ---- | ------ | ------------------------ |
| Certification slip | Dominant; twelve months defers ₹180–540 Cr per year at ramp | Staged certification beginning with a restricted category; named authority engagement; owned or guaranteed flight-test capacity |
| Order concentration | High; one domestic customer decides the decade | Engagement across navy, air force and army; export options that open after the reference fleet |
| Substitution by attritables or satellites | Medium; see the kill criteria below | Mission mix weighted to maritime and standoff roles; open-architecture roadmap that lets the aircraft absorb the attritables as payloads |
| Import-dependent BoM and FX | Medium | Indigenous sensor and datalink path; escalation clauses; a natural hedge once exports begin |
| Autonomy scrutiny and export licensing | Medium | Human-on-the-loop doctrine documented early; a compliance function before the first export campaign, since C5ISR payloads carry their own licensing regimes |
| Key people | Medium | Certification engineers and flight-test crews are the scarcest input after capital; check retention terms |

Beyond the register, four falsifiers. A thesis without kill criteria is a hope, so watch these fires and act if one flares:

1. **An attritable endurance breakthrough.** A sub-USD-1M airframe demonstrating 20-plus hours, 200-plus kilograms of multi-sensor payload and satellite communications at production scale would breach the physical moat in §4. Nothing in production today comes close. Check annually against demonstrated systems, never against announcements.
2. **Satellite substitution.** Proliferated low-orbit radar and RF constellations reaching sub-15-minute revisit with real-time tasking, at a cost per maintained track that closes the maritime gap, would erode the demand for airborne persistence. Track revisit rates and cost per track, and ignore the press releases.
3. **The standoff squeeze.** Long-range surface-to-air missiles proliferating to the point where safe standoff distance exceeds sensor utility even over open water would shrink the surviving mission set toward zero. Watch engagement ranges in published incidents.
4. **The procurement tell, the cleanest of the four.** India's 70 to 100 aircraft requirement has to materialise as tenders and follow-on orders by roughly 2028. If the follow-on money flows instead to small drones, high-altitude pseudo-satellites and satellites, the customer has answered the question this document asks, and the correct response is to exit rather than argue.

## Glossary

- **SCALE UAS**: Specialised C5ISR Autonomous Long Endurance unmanned aircraft system; the class this document defines in §1.
- **C5ISR**: Command, Control, Computers, Communications, Cyber, Intelligence, Surveillance and Reconnaissance.
- **MALE**: Medium-Altitude Long-Endurance; the legacy label for large drones of this weight class, which bundles the disrupted strike mission together with the durable C5ISR missions.
- **ISR**: Intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance.
- **SIGINT / EW**: Signals intelligence and electronic warfare: collecting, and disrupting, an adversary's radio and radar emissions.
- **MDA**: Maritime domain awareness: continuous surveillance of sea areas.
- **Attritable**: A drone cheap enough to lose in combat as a matter of course.
- **Mothership / launched effects**: A large aircraft carrying and directing expendable drones released mid-mission.
- **MUM-T**: Manned-unmanned teaming: crewed and uncrewed aircraft operating as one formation.
- **ASP**: Average selling price.
- **Backlog**: Signed orders not yet delivered or recognised as revenue.
- **Book-to-bill**: Orders booked divided by revenue recognised in a period; above 1 means the order book is growing.
- **BoM**: Bill of materials: the parts cost of a unit, before labour, integration, test and overheads.
- **NRE**: Non-recurring engineering: the one-time design, development and qualification cost.
- **CEMILAC / DGAQA**: India's military airworthiness design-certification and quality-assurance authorities.
- **DO-178C**: The governing standard for certifying airborne software.
- **HAPS**: High-altitude pseudo-satellite: a solar-powered stratospheric drone with weeks of endurance.
- **P-8I**: India's crewed maritime patrol aircraft, the manned system whose mission the SCALE class partially assumes at lower cost per hour.
